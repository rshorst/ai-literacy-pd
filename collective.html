The day · 2:45 to 3:15

From classroom choices to collective decisions

We bring together the questions that have surfaced throughout the day and consider what can be decided by an individual educator, what may need shared school guidance, and what calls for broader community conversation. We look at emerging approaches elsewhere as possibilities to examine, not templates to adopt.

What needs to be decided here, who needs to be part of that decision, and what should remain open?

Choose two or three policies

Six published K to 12 policies that take very different positions, here to examine, not to adopt. There are twenty in all.

Adds AI to the plagiarism rules

Seaquam Secondary School (Delta SD37), Academic Honesty

Delta, BC. Secondary, grades 8 to 12. November 2024. Governs students.

A school academic-integrity page that adds copying from AI, or using AI content without sourcing it, to the list of plagiarism offences. Consequences include a mark of zero, a call home and referral to administration. No guidance on permitted use, no disclosure format, no tool list.

Notable: The typical BC bolt-on: AI handled as a plagiarism category inside an existing honesty policy rather than as its own framework. A good contrast case.

Read the policy

No AI unless the teacher says so

Park East High School (NYC), AI and Academic Integrity Policies

New York. Grades 9 to 12. 2026–27 school year. Governs students.

Restrictive by default: every assignment is red (no AI) unless the teacher makes it yellow or green. Where AI is allowed, students disclose what it generated and how they used it. A consequence ladder runs from a call home to a redo under a strict timeframe, then a zero.

Notable: The clearest red-by-default policy, with a fully specified discipline ladder. A useful contrast to permissive-by-default models.

Read the policy

Optional, with a scale the teacher sets per task

Waterloo Region District School Board, GenAI Student Guidelines

Waterloo Region, Ontario. Grades 7 to 12. May 2026.

Two board-licensed tools for grades 7 and up, always optional and teacher-guided. Teachers can include, limit or leave out AI for each task using a four-level scale, with worked citation examples. Copying without citation is plagiarism.

Notable: A grade 7 floor with teacher opt-out, and a ready-made student-facing scale.

Read the policy

Tiered by age, with consent and an alternative

Halton District School Board, Generative AI Staff and Student Use Guidelines

Halton Region, Ontario. K to 12 with tiers. August 2025.

The clearest tiered model found: no student tools for K to 6, a short vetted list for grades 7 and 8 with signed parental consent and an alternate assignment for anyone without it, and a broader list for 9 to 12. Staff may use AI for planning and report-card drafting as a supplement to professional judgement, and must disclose AI-generated lesson content.

Notable: An explicit K to 6 prohibition, per-class consent letters and mandatory alternate assignments.

Read the policy

Conversations, not detectors

Canadian Rockies Public Schools, Administrative Procedure 140

Canmore and Banff, Alberta. K to 12 with grade 9 to 12 provisions. August 2026. Governs students and staff.

Folds AI into the technology acceptable-use procedure. Students may use AI to generate ideas, practise skills or deepen understanding, and must disclose it and mark which parts were AI-assisted. Only district-approved tools with privacy agreements may be used. Staff are steered away from AI detectors toward conversations with students. Deepfakes of any person without permission are banned.

Notable: An explicit anti-deepfake rule and an explicit stance against detection software, with the preferred alternative named.

Read the policy

A line on Indigenous content

Langley School District (SD35), AI Guidelines

Langley, BC. K to 12; Copilot for staff, student access planned for grades 9 to 12. Spring 2025.

Teacher-facing. Staff may use the district Copilot for planning, assignments, rubrics and family communications, and must tell students when and how AI is permitted. At the request of the district's Aboriginal Program, teachers are asked not to use AI to create Indigenous learning opportunities, which the guidelines name as cultural appropriation.

Notable: The only policy found that explicitly prohibits using AI to generate Indigenous learning content.

Ask these as you read

What it assumes

  • What does it assume about students, teachers and the tool?
  • Is AI treated as a cheating problem, a tool to manage, or something to learn about?
  • What counts as using AI here? Spellcheck, translation, search?

Who decides

  • Who wrote it, and who was asked?
  • Where do students, families and community appear, if at all?
  • Can a student or family say no? Is there a real alternative if they do?

What it leaves out

  • Data: where does student information go, and who can see it?
  • Cost and access: who pays, and who goes without?
  • Place: does it say anything about local or Indigenous knowledge, and who has permission to share it?

How it works in a classroom

  • What would a teacher actually do differently on Monday?
  • How does AI use become visible: disclosure, conversation, detection?
  • What happens when something goes wrong?

For us

  • What would we keep, what would we change, and what is missing for our place?
  • Who needs to be part of deciding here?
