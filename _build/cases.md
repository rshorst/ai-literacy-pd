# Classroom cases

## What would you do here?

The ten cases below are **fictional classroom adaptations grounded in published research or reporting**. Each source describes the evidence behind the dilemma, not a claim that the scenario itself occurred.

### Learning · Knowing · Creating · Relating

- **Learning:** What learning matters and what experiences make it possible?
- **Knowing:** What counts as understanding, evidence or trustworthy knowledge?
- **Creating:** What is being made, and how do authorship, experimentation and voice matter?
- **Relating:** Who and what are affected? What responsibilities, permissions and relationships matter?

**Across all four:** What is the machine doing? What could we redesign?

## Whole-room sample case

### Sample: The source looks real.

*Shared whole-room demonstration*

A Grade 7 teacher asks an AI assistant for a historical quotation and a source for a class inquiry. The response provides a compelling quotation, a named historical figure, and a plausible-sounding publication. The teacher is ready to include it in the lesson. A colleague tries to locate the publication and cannot find it. What should happen next? What changes if the publication can be found but the quotation is paraphrased or attributed to the wrong figure? What changes if it happens to be perfectly correct?

**Questions:** What counts as evidence that something is true? What does the apparent confidence of the output do to our judgment? How could this become a learning opportunity rather than only an error to correct?

**Source context:** UNESCO, Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research (2023) cautions that fluent AI-generated text can be false and requires human validation. The quotation-and-source incident here is a fictional demonstration of that risk.

[Read the source](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research)

## Ten group cases

### Case 01: But I wrote it myself.

*Academic integrity and trust*

A secondary student submits a noticeably stronger essay than usual. A teacher runs it through an AI detector, which flags the work. The student insists they wrote it and offers to explain their ideas. The teacher knows that some students have submitted generated work and wants assessment to be fair. Another teacher suggests an in-class rewrite. The student is distressed that improving seems to have made them suspicious.

**Questions:** What would count as good evidence of the student’s learning and authorship? How could the first conversation protect both fairness and trust?

**Source context:** Lee V. Gaines, NPR/KQED (16 Dec 2025): report of a Maryland student disputing accusations of AI use. This scenario is adapted, not the reported student’s complete story.

[Read the source](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/66031/teachers-are-using-software-to-see-if-students-used-ai-what-happens-when-its-wrong)

### Case 02: It sounds better, but not like me.

*Writing, voice, authorship*

A student asks AI to revise a personal essay. The new version is fluent, neatly structured, and more aligned with the rubric. It also removes some of the student’s humour, doubt, and expressions. The student thinks the polished version might earn a higher mark. Their friend says it no longer sounds like them. The teacher has permitted AI for editing, but has not discussed where editing becomes something else.

**Questions:** What learning happens through composing and revising? Who decides what “better” writing means? What choices should the student be able to explain?

**Source context:** Levine, Beck, Mah, Phalen & Pittman, Journal of Adolescent & Adult Literacy (first online 2024; issue 2025). Small qualitative study of 12 high-school writers using ChatGPT for argument writing; findings included comparison with AI text as a way to identify voice.

[Read the source](https://doi.org/10.1002/jaal.1373)

### Case 03: I understand it when the chatbot explains it.

*Mathematics and independent understanding*

Students use an AI chatbot while practising mathematics. Many finish the exercises and say the explanations help. A week later, some struggle with similar problems without it. One student says: “I understood each step while I was reading it. I don’t know why I can’t start this one myself.” The teacher has to decide what kinds of help to offer and how to recognize developing understanding.

**Questions:** What is the difference between following a worked solution and being able to reason? What kinds of scaffolding help students become more capable?

**Source context:** Bastani et al., PNAS (2025). Randomized high-school mathematics study found unrestricted AI assistance improved practice performance but lowered later unaided performance relative to controls; a guarded tutor reduced that problem.

[Read the source](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)

### Case 04: Did you actually read my work?

*AI feedback and relationships*

An overworked teacher uses AI to draft comments on student essays. They read the work and review the generated comments before returning them. Feedback arrives quickly and is generally specific. One student asks: “Did you really read this?” Another appreciates getting advice in time to revise. The teacher wonders what students expect from feedback beyond useful suggestions.

**Questions:** Is feedback only information that improves work? What forms of attention, recognition, and trust should it also carry?

**Source context:** Doyle, Nash, Jakcsiova & Turner, Technology, Knowledge and Learning (2025). Interviews with 12 teachers and focus groups with 9 secondary students identified efficiencies and relational concerns; not a causal trial of learning outcomes.

[Read the source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10758-025-09903-0)

### Case 05: We wanted to make something about this place.

*Cultural representation and permission*

Students create an illustrated booklet about the place where they live and use an AI image generator for depictions of local Indigenous culture. Some images appear plausible but include invented details. The teacher values the students’ enthusiasm, yet no appropriate cultural authority has been consulted. A student suggests labelling the pictures “AI-generated” and going ahead. The booklet is almost ready to share with families.

**Questions:** Does a label resolve the concern? Whose knowledge and authority matter? What would a different process of making this resource look like?

**Source context:** Emily Blake, Cabin Radio (25 May 2026). Reporting on criticism of AI illustrations in a Northwest Territories Mining Week activity book, including concerns raised by a northern artist and teacher. The classroom booklet is fictional.

[Read the source](https://cabinradio.ca/291932/news/arts/ai-generated-activity-book-for-nwt-mining-week-draws-concerns/)

### Case 06: It’s obviously fake. Why does it matter?

*Consent, likeness and respect*

A student uses a free app to turn a teacher’s yearbook photo into a short video of the teacher dancing and lip-syncing to a pop song. It goes around the class group chat. It isn’t mean, and most students think it’s funny, including some who like the teacher. The teacher hears about it from a student and feels uneasy, but doesn’t want to overreact. The student who made it says everyone knows it’s fake and it was only a joke.

**Questions:** Does it matter that it’s obviously fake, or that it isn’t mean? What is owed to someone before their face or voice is used? How could this become a conversation about consent and not only a discipline issue?

**Source context:** WIRED (March 2026). Reporting on student-run “slander pages” that use AI video tools such as Viggle AI to put teachers’ faces into mocking clips, many of them far crueller than this. The dancing video is a fictional, milder adaptation.

[Read the source](https://www.wired.com/story/teens-are-using-ai-fueled-slander-pages-to-mock-their-teachers/)

### Case 07: Copilot made this lesson in thirty seconds.

*Planning and educational purpose*

A teacher asks Copilot to create a social studies lesson. The result includes an objective, activities, assessment, and extension. It looks excellent and saves time. During the lesson, however, students mostly recall facts and repeat familiar explanations. A student asks a difficult question about power and competing interpretations, but the planned activities offer little space for investigation. The teacher wonders what makes a lesson genuinely good.

**Questions:** What learning was the lesson supposed to make possible? Which parts of planning require professional and contextual judgment? How might the lesson be redesigned?

**Source context:** Trust and colleagues, CITE Journal (2025). Analysis of 310 AI-generated civics lesson plans (ChatGPT, Gemini and Copilot) found recurring formulaic structures and limited higher-order thinking opportunities.

[Read the source](https://citejournal.org/volume-25/issue-3-25/social-studies/civic-education-in-the-age-of-ai-should-we-trust-ai-generated-lesson-plans/)

### Case 08: AI could help us write better learning plans.

*Privacy, representation and disability*

A learning-support teacher uses AI to organize notes and suggest accommodations for a student. The resulting plan has useful ideas but emphasizes difficulties more than strengths and repeats outdated information. The teacher has entered detailed information into the tool and is unsure what the school’s privacy arrangements permit. A colleague asks how the student and family will be involved.

**Questions:** What does it mean to know a learner? How can support avoid reducing someone to a deficit summary? What information should never be entered without permission and safeguards?

**Source context:** Ariana Aboulafia, Center for Democracy & Technology (28 Oct 2025). U.S. brief on generative AI in individualized education programs, discussing potential benefits and risks in accuracy, bias, privacy and individualization. U.S. law does not describe BC requirements.

[Read the source](https://cdt.org/insights/from-personalized-to-programmed-the-use-of-generative-ai-to-develop-individualized-education-programs-for-students-with-disabilities/)

### Case 09: The chatbot understands me better.

*Belonging and companionship*

A usually quiet student tells a trusted teacher that they talk to an AI companion every evening. The system is always available, responds gently, and seems less judgmental than peers. The student has begun avoiding some conversations with friends because the chatbot feels easier. The teacher wants to take seriously the comfort the student experiences without allowing an artificial relationship to replace other needed support.

**Questions:** What does feeling understood mean? How could a supportive response respect the student while strengthening connections with people?

**Source context:** Common Sense Media, Talk, Trust, and Trade-Offs (16 July 2025). U.S. teen survey documenting AI-companion use and personal disclosures; it does not establish that using an AI companion causes social isolation.

[Read the source](https://about.commonsensemedia.org/research/reports/talk-trust-and-trade-offs-how-and-why-teens-use-ai-companions)

### Case 10: I do not want to use AI.

*Participation, choice and governance*

A school encourages Copilot use in research and planning. One teacher designs an assignment that requires students to experiment with it. Several students object for different reasons: environmental costs, privacy, and a desire to practise without automation. The teacher proposes that they work with classmates who are willing to operate Copilot, but the students say that does not address their concerns.

**Questions:** What would meaningful participation or refusal look like? Who gets to decide whether an activity requires AI? How might equivalent learning opportunities be designed?

**Source context:** Katie Hyslop, The Tyee (28 May 2026). Reporting on proposed Copilot accounts for Vancouver students and student objections concerning environment, safety, privacy and learning. Classroom dispute above is fictional.

[Read the source](https://thetyee.ca/News/2026/05/28/AI-Chatbots-Coming-BC-Classrooms/)

## Copilot experiments
Choose one. First discuss and record an initial response with your group. Only paste fictional scenario text and your own group ideas.

### 1. Challenge Our Thinking

```text
We are educators discussing a fictional classroom dilemma. Situation: [PASTE CASE]. Our initial response: [OUR THINKING]. Do not decide for us. Identify one assumption about learning, knowing, creating or relating; a plausible perspective we have overlooked; and two questions worth discussing. Do not invent missing facts. Under 180 words.
```

### 2. Imagine Something Different

```text
Situation: [PASTE CASE]. Our initial response: [OUR THINKING]. Suggest three substantively different ways to redesign the learning activity, assessment, available help, relationships or room. Include one without AI. For each, state what it makes possible and what it might compromise. Do not assume AI is needed. Under 220 words.
```

### 3. Interrogate The Machine

```text
Situation: [PASTE CASE]. Recommend what a teacher might do in fewer than 120 words. Then identify which missing details or assumptions limit the recommendation. We will critically examine your answer.
```
