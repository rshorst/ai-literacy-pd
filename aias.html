11:25 · How should AI fit into classroom work?

What kind of task is this?

The AI Assessment Scale, first introduced by Leon Furze in 2023 and developed with Mike Perkins, Jasper Roe and Jason MacVaugh, names five kinds of task, not five levels of permission. No level is better than another.

Download the handout (PDF)The AI Assessment Scale site ↗
LevelThe task is…For example: a Grade 9 argument on a local issue
1 No AIDone where AI can’t reach: in the room, on paper, out loud.Write the argument in class, by hand, from your own notes.
2 AI planningAbout planning, which is assessed whether or not AI was used.Use AI to find counterarguments. You’re assessed on the outline you build.
3 AI collaborationBuilt so AI alone can’t meet the standard. You assess what the student does with it.Draft it yourself, get AI feedback, then mark which suggestions you took or refused, and why.
4 Full AIToo big for AI or a person alone. You assess the directing.Direct AI to write versions for two audiences. You’re assessed on your instructions and your choice.
5 AI explorationA new use, designed with students.As a class, invent a use. For example, AI plays the people affected at a mock town hall.

Questions for the scale

  1. Real work is messy. Where does your task slip between levels?
  2. Who decides the level, and can students question it?
  3. Can a “No AI” task keep AI out? What does enforcing it cost, and who pays?
  4. Where does a student with an IEP, a shared device or another first language fit?
  5. At levels 4 and 5, what happens to a student who says no?
  6. What does sorting tasks this way leave out: data, cost, place, relationships?

Assess the process

If AI can make the product, look at how the work was made, not only the end result.

  1. 1Plan

    A quick plan, question list or outline, made in class.

    Look for: What did they decide to do, and why?

  2. 2Draft

    Drafts saved along the way, with a note on what changed.

    Look for: How did the thinking move?

  3. 3Decide

    An AI statement: what I used, kept and refused, and why. “None” is an answer.

    Look for: Where were the student’s own choices?

  4. 4Talk

    Two minutes: What did you decide, and why? Where did you change your mind?

    Look for: Can they explain it in their own words?

Watch for: a conversation, not a trap. Process evidence can turn into surveillance.
