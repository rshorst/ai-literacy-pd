Positions to argue with

Policy examples

Twenty published K to 12 AI policies, ten secondary and ten elementary, from BC, the rest of Canada, the US, the UK and Australia. They are here as positions to argue with, not templates to adopt.

Read in September 2026. Policies in this area change quickly, so check the link before putting one in front of a room.

Two ways to use them

Critique. Give small groups three contrasting documents, such as Seaquam's bolt-on, Park East's red-by-default and Waterloo's scale. Read each through the dimensions: what does it assume about data and where it goes, about authorship and relation, whose labour does it move, and what future does it take for granted? Then: which would you want to teach under, and what would you add?

Build your own. Use the lists as positions to argue with while writing your own guidance. Langley's line on Indigenous content, Central Okanagan's framing of consent as a legal duty, Halton's tiers, Peel's supervised-not-prohibited stance and Wyndham's equity rule are good starting points. For a class version, see the class AI agreement.

For the afternoon, six positions to argue with sets six of these side by side with questions for groups, and Draft your AI guidance is the worksheet for your own first draft.

Secondary

Seaquam Secondary School (Delta SD37), Academic Honesty

Delta, BC. Secondary, grades 8 to 12. November 2024. Governs students.

A school academic-integrity page that adds copying from AI, or using AI content without sourcing it, to the list of plagiarism offences. Consequences include a mark of zero, a call home and referral to administration. No guidance on permitted use, no disclosure format, no tool list.

Notable: The typical BC bolt-on: AI handled as a plagiarism category inside an existing honesty policy rather than as its own framework. A good contrast case.

Read the policy

School District No. 5 (Southeast Kootenay), Policy 24: Generative Artificial Intelligence

Cranbrook and Fernie, BC. District K to 12. Adopted May 2024. Governs students, staff and others.

A formal board-adopted governance policy covering how AI tools are acquired and used: transparency, human verification of every output, bias evaluation of tools, training before deployment, privacy compliance and non-discrimination. Principles rather than classroom rules; the operational detail sits in an administrative procedure.

Notable: One of the few BC districts with an adopted board policy, not just guidelines. Shows the governance layer above classroom practice.

Read the policy

York Region District School Board, Student AI Guidelines (Grades 7 to 12)

York Region, Ontario. Grades 7 to 12. January 2026. Governs students, with a companion page for educators.

Permissive but structured. Board-approved tools may be used for brainstorming, tutoring, study notes, proofreading and organising research, always with the teacher's permission for each assignment. Disclosure and citation in the bibliography are mandatory, and students stay responsible for everything they submit. The educator page adds that student work should not be entered into AI for assessment.

Notable: Pairs a student page with an educator page so the obligations mirror each other.

Read the policy

Canadian Rockies Public Schools, Administrative Procedure 140

Canmore and Banff, Alberta. K to 12 with grade 9 to 12 provisions. August 2026. Governs students and staff.

Folds AI into the technology acceptable-use procedure. Students may use AI to generate ideas, practise skills or deepen understanding, and must disclose it and mark which parts were AI-assisted. Only district-approved tools with privacy agreements may be used. Staff are steered away from AI detectors toward conversations with students. Deepfakes of any person without permission are banned.

Notable: An explicit anti-deepfake rule and an explicit stance against detection software, with the preferred alternative named.

Read the policy

Snohomish High School, AI Code of Conduct

Snohomish, Washington. Grades 9 to 12. October 2024. Governs students.

Teachers label each assignment AI recommended, AI permitted or AI restricted. The default is no use without teacher approval; attribution is required, no personal information may be entered, and misuse may be treated as cheating.

Notable: The clearest school-level traffic-light scheme.

Read the policy

Park East High School (NYC), AI and Academic Integrity Policies

New York. Grades 9 to 12. 2026–27 school year. Governs students.

Restrictive by default: every assignment is red (no AI) unless the teacher makes it yellow or green. Where AI is allowed, students disclose what it generated and how they used it. A consequence ladder runs from a call home to a redo under a strict timeframe, then a zero.

Notable: The clearest red-by-default policy, with a fully specified discipline ladder. A useful contrast to permissive-by-default models.

Read the policy

Wyndham College (NSW), Generative AI in assessment

Quakers Hill, New South Wales. Years 11 and 12. September 2024. Governs students, with teacher duties.

Every assessment notification declares one of four levels, from no AI to sanctioned tools generating content. Students keep evidence of their AI use and cite it; suspected malpractice goes through a review panel with a right of appeal. Tasks can never require AI, because that would conflict with equity.

Notable: Combines a scale with an equity rule and a due-process chain.

Read the policy

Harristown State High School, Artificial Intelligence policy

Toowoomba, Queensland. Years 7 to 12. July 2025. Governs students, with teacher duties.

AI is allowed for brainstorming, research, clarifying concepts, flashcards and summaries, and banned for anything the marking guide assesses. Students keep and supply their prompt transcripts. Senior work suspected of prohibited use is not marked.

Notable: Ties the line between allowed and prohibited to the marking guide itself, and uses prompt transcripts as evidence rather than detectors.

Read the policy

Tudor Grange Academies Trust, AI Policy

West Midlands and Worcestershire, England. Primary and secondary. April 2026. Governs staff and pupils.

Staff-focused. Authorised tools only; AI may draft resources, mark objective items and help write reports, with human review and disclosure of where AI was used. A data protection assessment is required before AI note-takers, and emotion-recognition AI is banned. Pupils may use authorised, age-appropriate tools.

Notable: Treats teacher and administrative AI use as the main governance risk.

Read the policy

Gwinnett County Public Schools, Guidance for Human-Centered AI Use

Gwinnett County, Georgia. District K to 12. September 2025. Governs students, staff and parents.

Principle-based guidance from a large, AI-forward district: humans in the loop for all uses, encouraged student uses, a definition of AI plagiarism, citation when allowed, and staff instructions never to enter personal information or rely on unreviewed output. Using AI to impersonate, bully or harass is banned.

Notable: Treats AI-enabled bullying and impersonation as a conduct issue, not only an integrity issue.

Read the policy

Elementary

BC Ministry of Education and Child Care, What you need to know about AI (Elementary)

British Columbia. Provincial, one of three versions. Written directly to children.

A one-page set of norms for children rather than a rule book: AI as a helper, not a replacement for your own thinking; add your own ideas; double-check everything; protect personal information; ask your teacher; tell a trusted adult if something feels wrong. A companion parent sheet comes in eight languages.

Notable: The only provincial document in Canada written in age-differentiated language for elementary children. Several BC districts reproduce it.

Read the policy

Vancouver School Board, AI guidelines and Copilot 13+

Vancouver, BC. K to 12; the age gate excludes elementary. May 2026.

Copilot 13+ is available inside the district tenant only to secondary students aged 13 and older, so elementary use is teacher-mediated. Educator guidelines permit planning, feedback and differentiation, with review of AI output, disclosure to students and no identifiable student data. The student guidelines were developed with educators and secondary students.

Notable: The most current BC example of a hard no-accounts-under-13 line. A parent-led campaign is contesting it, which makes it good discussion material.

Read the policy

Langley School District (SD35), AI Guidelines

Langley, BC. K to 12; Copilot for staff, student access planned for grades 9 to 12. Spring 2025.

Teacher-facing. Staff may use the district Copilot for planning, assignments, rubrics and family communications, and must tell students when and how AI is permitted. At the request of the district's Aboriginal Program, teachers are asked not to use AI to create Indigenous learning opportunities, which the guidelines name as cultural appropriation.

Notable: The only policy found that explicitly prohibits using AI to generate Indigenous learning content.

Read the policy

Central Okanagan Public Schools (SD23), Guidelines for the Responsible Use of Generative AI

Kelowna, BC. K to 12. April 2025. Governs educators, students and parents.

Puts the onus on teachers: check the approved tools list, respect vendor age minimums, judge developmental appropriateness for younger students, and send a parent consent form when data leaves Canada. Recommends against AI writing detectors.

Notable: Frames age limits and parental consent as legal obligations under FIPPA, and rejects detectors outright.

Read the policy

Halton District School Board, Generative AI Staff and Student Use Guidelines

Halton Region, Ontario. K to 12 with tiers. August 2025.

The clearest tiered model found: no student tools for K to 6, a short vetted list for grades 7 and 8 with signed parental consent and an alternate assignment for anyone without it, and a broader list for 9 to 12. Staff may use AI for planning and report-card drafting as a supplement to professional judgement, and must disclose AI-generated lesson content.

Notable: An explicit K to 6 prohibition, per-class consent letters and mandatory alternate assignments.

Read the policy

Waterloo Region District School Board, GenAI Student Guidelines

Waterloo Region, Ontario. Grades 7 to 12. May 2026.

Two board-licensed tools for grades 7 and up, always optional and teacher-guided. Teachers can include, limit or leave out AI for each task using a four-level scale, with worked citation examples. Copying without citation is plagiarism.

Notable: A grade 7 floor with teacher opt-out, and a ready-made student-facing scale.

Read the policy

Peel District School Board, Interim Guidance on Generative AI

Peel Region, Ontario. K to 12 with an under-13 provision. May 2024, reaffirmed December 2025.

Rather than banning, Peel allows students under 13 to use generative AI only with supervision, and all students only with educator approval. Staff review all AI output, attribute it, and keep personal data out.

Notable: A supervised-not-prohibited stance for under-13s. A useful contrast to Halton.

Read the policy

Joy Lane Primary School, Acceptable Use of Generative AI

Whitstable, Kent, England. Ages 4 to 11. March 2026.

Pupils may use AI only when supervised by an adult, as a research tool, or when studying AI itself; never in assessments or for homework passed off as their own. Staff may use approved tools for planning, resources, marking support and parent letters, and must keep personal data out of prompts.

Notable: A complete, current primary policy with a named list of approved tools.

Read the policy

Manly Village Public School (NSW), Generative AI Implementation Procedures

Manly, New South Wales. K to 6; AI limited to Years 5 and 6. 2025.

Prohibited by default, with one exception: Years 5 and 6 may use the state-built, filtered chatbot under teacher supervision. No AI for homework or assessment.

Notable: A school layering stricter procedures on top of a state-provided tool.

Read the policy

Central Elementary School (CCSD 62), AI in Education

Des Plaines, Illinois. K to 8 district. September 2025.

Permissive but teacher-controlled: two vetted tools approved for teacher-directed activities, contracts that restrict data collection from under-13s and bar vendors from training on student data, AI literacy lessons at every grade, and a signed student expectations document.

Notable: A family-facing page that names the tools, the legal basis for under-13 protection and the permitted uses.

Patterns across the twenty

  • Disclosure and citation is the near-universal student rule. Few say what a good disclosure looks like; Waterloo and York give worked examples.
  • Teacher discretion, task by task, is the default mechanism. Scales make it visible: Waterloo's and Wyndham's four levels, Snohomish's and Park East's traffic lights. Park East is the only one that names the default.
  • Age gates do most of the elementary work. Thirteen is the usual line, or a grade floor. Peel is the exception: supervised use rather than prohibition.
  • No personal information goes into the tool, in almost every document, for students and staff alike.
  • Teacher use is governed less often than student use. When it is, the rules are approved tools only, review every output, disclose AI-generated materials, and never enter student work for assessment.
  • Consequences range from nothing stated to bolt-on plagiarism penalties, progressive ladders and due-process chains.
  • Detectors: three documents reject them, and none endorse them.
  • Equity appears once as a rule: Wyndham says AI can never be required for a task.
  • Indigenous knowledge is named only by Langley. Nothing else in the twenty touches knowledge sovereignty.
  • Labour, environment and cost are almost absent, and none ask what futures the policy assumes.

Every policy here is about permission and detection. None is about what AI does to the conditions of meaning-making in a classroom. That gap is where an entangled policy starts.
