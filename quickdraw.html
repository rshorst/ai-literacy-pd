Quick, Draw!

You get a word and 20 seconds to draw it. While you draw, a neural network guesses what it is.

Play Quick, Draw! ↗Opens in a new tab. Google doesn't let the game run inside other sites.

Why we play it

It is a quick way to watch machine learning work, with no account needed. It also shows what that learning takes: millions of people drawing for free, and a system that learns the most common way to draw a thing.

How it learns

The game does not see your drawing the way a person would. It compares your strokes with the patterns in drawings other players made for the same word. Google Creative Lab launched it in 2016, and it has collected over a billion drawings across 345 words. Each one was saved with the word, the order of the strokes, whether the game guessed it, and the player’s country.

What gets lost or left out

  • The usual way wins. When Google looked at more than 115,000 shoe drawings, one style that looks like a sneaker dominated. The game struggled to recognize high heels or sandals as shoes.
  • Culture is in the strokes. In the data, 86% of circles drawn in the US went counterclockwise, while 80% of circles drawn in Japan went clockwise. Players in Korea and Russia were more likely to draw a chair sideways.
  • Someone chose the words. The game asks for 345 things that can be drawn in 20 seconds. Whatever is not on the list, or is drawn differently here, is not what the system learns.
  • Odd drawings get corrected. A drawing network trained on these doodles, given a cat with three eyes, draws it back with two.

What Google did with the drawings

In 2017 Google released 50 million of them as an open dataset, with each player’s country and the time of each drawing. Its researchers trained Sketch-RNN, a network that draws and can finish your doodles, on millions of them. Google also used the dataset to study how representative large datasets are, which is where the shoe finding comes from.

Games that train machines

Getting people to play so a system can learn is an old move. In 2004 the ESP Game paired strangers to type words for the same image, and Google licensed it as the Google Image Labeler to improve image search. reCAPTCHA, which Google bought in 2009, had people type words from scanned books and newspapers to prove they were human, which helped digitize Google Books. From 2012 it showed house numbers from Street View, and later it asked people to click on crosswalks and traffic lights.

To talk about

  • Who did the work in this game, and who benefits from it?
  • What did the game reward you for drawing?
  • What would it fail to recognize from here?
